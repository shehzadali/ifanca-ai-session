Research across North American Muslim communities and the broader Muslim world reveals a complex mix of religious concerns, institutional distrust, and viral misinformation driving resistance to immunization and points to where credible Islamic institutions can make a difference. Although the COVID-19 pandemic has largely faded from daily headlines, one of its most enduring legacies has been a deep erosion of public trust in vaccines and health institutions — a challenge that, for Muslim communities, carries the additional dimension of halal compliance and religious permissibility. When a prominent Muslim cleric declares the Covid-19 vaccine haram, the video spreads faster than any public health message can counter it. “In North America, hesitancy is shaped by post-9/11 distrust, historical grievances, and the same information ecosystems that have fueled anti-vaccination sentiment across the broader population. Questions of halal compliance add another layer that demands clear, authoritative guidance from credible Islamic institutions.” Answering the Halal Question The most distinctly Muslim dimension of vaccine hesitancy is the question of halal status. Several widely distributed vaccines use, or are alleged to use, pork-derived gelatin as a stabilizing agent, a substance prohibited under Islamic law. Aishah Holley-Clark, a Muslim woman in the United States, cited halal concerns among her reasons for declining vaccination. “There are questions as to whether it is halal or not because almost all vaccines are pork gelatin-based,” she said in “What’s Behind the COVID-19 Vaccine Hesitancy Among Muslim Americans.” The concern is not specific to North America and has roots that go beyond the pandemic. In India, clerics declared certain COVID vaccines impermissible, while scholars across Asia issued rulings against the Chinese-manufactured vaccine, according to reporting by the Associated Press and an article published by The Hastings Center, a bioethics research institute. Malaysia offers the starkest illustration of where unresolved halal uncertainty leads: the halal status of vaccines has been identified as the single biggest obstacle to vaccination among Muslim parents there, ultimately forcing the government to make childhood vaccination compulsory, with fines and jail time for non-compliance. The mainstream scholarly position, as Dr. Harunor Rashid of the University of Sydney noted in the same article, is that the principle of darura — necessity — renders vaccines permissible when the alternative is greater harm. But when dissenting voices are amplified on social media and unified institutional guidance is absent, that consensus struggles to reach the people who need it most. This is precisely where organizations such as IFANCA play a uniquely valuable role. “By combining halal certification expertise with scientific evaluation of pharmaceutical ingredients, IFANCA helps answer questions that neither healthcare providers nor religious leaders can address independently — transforming uncertainty into informed decision-making.” Institutional Distrust: A Specifically American Shape Beyond theological concerns lies a harder problem: institutional distrust with a specific historical texture that sets the North American Muslim community apart from the broader population. Daniel Haqiqatjou, a thought leader in Islamic discourse in the United States and founder of the Aslana Institute, framed Muslim hesitancy in explicitly post-9/11 terms in the same article.  “Muslims have little trust in anything driven by government, corporate, or media interests,” he said. “Since 9/11, these ‘experts’ have come together to push a ‘global war on terror’ based on fear created by the false demonization of Islam and Muslims. Experts can be very wrong, and countless people suffer. We’ve witnessed that firsthand.” African American Muslims added a further layer of historical grievance. In the same article, Abdul Kabir Muhammad, a formerly incarcerated African American Muslim from Cincinnati, cited deep resentment toward American institutions “for the atrocities they are guilty of in dealing with us like the Tuskegee experiment” — a reference to the decades-long government study in which Black American men were deliberately denied treatment for syphilis. Woven through both forms of distrust is a specific suspicion of pharmaceutical companies. Research findings show that the belief that the vaccine rollout was primarily “a ploy by pharmaceutical companies to make money” featured prominently among hesitant respondents — a concern that became self-reinforcing as manufacturers were granted legal immunity from liability, a protection flagged repeatedly in online anti-vaccination communities. These are not concerns that a government press conference or a pharmaceutical company’s own reassurance will resolve. They require trusted messengers within the community itself, and those messengers must have earned their credibility over time, on issues that have nothing to do with vaccines. The Misinformation Ecosystem Social media accelerated the spread of misinformation into Muslim communities. On YouTube, an Australian creator branded imams who endorsed vaccination as complicit with dictatorship in a video titled “A Message to Aussie Muslims: Stand Up, Follow the Way of the Prophets,” while posts on Reddit’s r/PublicFreakout falsely tied the vaccine to infertility and toxic ingredients including formaldehyde and heavy metals. Perhaps the most damaging claim to circulate was that the vaccine causes female sterilization by attacking the placental protein syncytin-1 — originating in an article in Health & Money News under a falsely attributed “Head of Pfizer Research” headline before spreading globally, and gaining particular traction in Muslim communities due to Islam’s emphasis on family and reproduction. Most of this misinformation didn’t originate within Muslim communities. It came from the broader anti-vaccine ecosystem. That reframes the problem as appropriation of religious language to launder existing misinformation, meaning the fix isn’t theological debate but proactive science communication and trusted community partnerships. What Muslim-Majority Countries Show Us Vaccine hesitancy in Muslim-majority countries shares many of the same drivers, though local conditions shape the mix. In Pakistan, a survey conducted by Gallup Pakistan and the Pakistan Islamic Medical Association found 30% of respondents unwilling to vaccinate, with safety concerns cited by 54% of hesitant respondents. Notably, the same survey found this reluctance cut evenly across education and income levels: 67% of those with higher education said they would vaccinate, barely higher than the 63% of those with lower education levels who said the same. “Religious concerns seem to be a minor factor,” concluded the researchers behind the Gallup Pakistan and PIMA survey, but the conspiracy theories filling that space were no less corrosive. In sub-Saharan Africa, the picture is different again. When European governments paused the AstraZeneca vaccine over concerns about rare blood clots, that news spread instantly to Malawi and South Sudan, where AstraZeneca was the primary vaccine available. “You know it’s a global village,” Zaindi, a CARE aid worker in Malawi, told NPR. “When information is generated in Europe, it takes seconds to reach Africa. And that discouraged many of our young people.” He reported that only 40% of his own CARE staff had been vaccinated, so deep was the distrust. The Role of IFANCA in Strengthening Vaccine Confidence According to the same Gallup Pakistan and PIMA survey, just 5% of hesitant respondents said a political leader’s public vaccination would change their mind. Religious leaders fared only marginally better, at 9%, and only where religious authority is coherent and trusted. What works is more granular: trusted community voices, peer communication, and institutions that have built credibility on Islamic grounds over years rather than emerging only in a public health crisis. Accumulated trust is what makes a community willing to hear a difficult message. For Muslim communities, organizations such as IFANCA represent an important part of that trusted ecosystem. Future outbreaks and new pharmaceutical technologies will generate fresh questions, and preparing for them requires institutions capable of fostering trust before the next crisis arrives — ensuring that scientific evidence and Islamic principles work together rather than appear in conflict.
