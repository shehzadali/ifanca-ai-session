Clinical Observations from a Diabetes Medical Camp in Skardu, Gilgit-Baltistan, Pakistan Insulin resistance has emerged as one of the defining metabolic challenges facing South Asian populations. Across the region, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus has increased at an alarming rate over the past two decades, making South Asia home to one of the largest populations of people living with diabetes. Unlike many Western populations, “South Asians develop insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes at younger ages and at considerably lower body mass indices (BMI),” reflecting a unique combination of genetic susceptibility, body fat distribution, and lifestyle factors. Pakistan is no exception. The country has witnessed a dramatic rise in diabetes prevalence, with increasing numbers of patients presenting with poor glycemic control and diabetes-related complications. While metropolitan cities have received considerable research attention, relatively little is known about the metabolic health of populations living in the mountainous regions of Gilgit-Baltistan. Traditionally, these communitieswere characterized by physically demanding occupations, active lifestyles, and diets based largely on locally produced foods. However, changing dietary habits, urbanization, and reduced physical activity are gradually transforming the health profile of the region. A diabetes medical camp organized at the Regional Headquarters (RHQ) Hospital in Skardu on May 14, 2026, provided an opportunity to observe these changing trends firsthand. Although the camp was not designed as a population-based epidemiological study, the clinical findings offer valuable insight into the growing burden of insulin resistance and poor glycemic control among diabetic patients in northern Pakistan. During the medical camp, 36 patients with previously diagnosed diabetes mellitus underwent clinical assessment. Demographic characteristics, treatment regimens, body mass index, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and dietary habits were documented to evaluate their metabolic status. Among the participants, 21 (58.3%) were male, and 15 (41.7%) were female. Most patients were receiving oral hypoglycemic medications, while a smaller number required insulin therapy. Despite ongoing treatment, glycemic control remained poor in a substantial proportion of patients. 23 individuals (63.9%) had HbA1c levels exceeding 9 percent, indicating persistently elevated blood glucose concentrations over the preceding 3 months. Such levels are associated with a markedly increased risk of diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, cardiovascular disease, and other long-term complications. Another notable observation was the prevalence of excess body weight. Half of the participants had a BMI greater than 27 kg/m². While this threshold may not constitute severe obesity by conventional international standards, it carries far greater metabolic significance in South Asian populations. “Numerous studies have demonstrated that South Asians accumulate disproportionately greater amounts of visceral adipose tissue, even when their BMI falls within ranges traditionally considered normal or only mildly elevated.” Visceral fat is metabolically active and plays a central role in promoting chronic inflammation, impaired insulin signaling, and progressive insulin resistance. Dietary assessment further reinforced these observations. Many participants reported frequent consumption of refined flour products, white rice, sweetened beverages, confectionery, and other high glycemic-index foods. These dietary patterns contribute to repeated postprandial glucose spikes, excessive insulin secretion, and gradual deterioration of insulin sensitivity. Combined with reduced physical activity and increasing sedentary lifestyles, such nutritional habits create an environment that strongly favors the development and progression of insulin resistance. Insulin resistance represents the underlying metabolic defect in most patients with type 2 diabetes. Under normal physiological conditions, insulin facilitates glucose uptake into skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver. When tissues become resistant to insulin, the pancreas compensates by producing larger quantities of the hormone. Over time, pancreatic beta cells become unable to meet this increasing demand, leading to sustained hyperglycemia and progression to overt diabetes. The susceptibility of South Asians to insulin resistance has been consistently documented across multiple international studies. Compared with many other ethnic groups, South Asians possess a greater tendency toward abdominal adiposity, reduced lean muscle mass, altered adipokine profiles, and increased hepatic fat accumulation. These characteristics explain why diabetes frequently develops at lower BMI thresholds than those used to define obesity in Western populations. Consequently, reliance on BMI alone may underestimate metabolic risk among South Asians, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive metabolic screening. Although insulin resistance was not measured directly during the Skardu medical camp using methods such as the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) or the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique, the coexistence of poor glycemic control, elevated BMI, and high consumption of refined carbohydrates strongly suggests that insulin resistance is a major contributor to the metabolic abnormalities observed in this patient population. The observations from this clinical camp also reflect broader nutritional and lifestyle transitions occurring throughout Gilgit-Baltistan. Traditional diets rich in whole grains, legumes, locally grown vegetables, and seasonal fruits are increasingly being replaced by processed foods high in refined carbohydrates and added sugars. Simultaneously, mechanization, urbanization, and changing occupational patterns have reduced routine physical activity. Together, these changes are creating conditions that favor obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes. While the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the relatively small sample size and clinic-based nature of the observations, they nonetheless highlight an emerging public health concern deserving further investigation. Larger community-based studies incorporating direct measures of insulin resistance, waist circumference, visceral adiposity, dietary intake, and physical activity are needed to determine the true burden of metabolic disease in Gilgit-Baltistan. Equally important is the implementation of preventive strategies tailored to the unique needs of mountain communities. “Early metabolic screening, culturally appropriate nutrition education, promotion of physical activity, weight management programs, and regular HbA1c monitoring should become integral components of diabetes care throughout the region.” Given the heightened susceptibility of South Asians to insulin resistance, interventions should begin before the onset of overt diabetes, particularly among individuals with overweight status or a family history of metabolic disease. The clinical observations from Skardu serve as an important reminder that insulin resistance is no longer confined to urban populations. Even communities once considered relatively protected are experiencing the consequences of rapid nutritional and lifestyle transition. Addressing insulin resistance through early detection, public health education, and evidence-based preventive strategies will be essential for reducing the future burden of diabetes and its complications across Gilgit-Baltistan and the wider South Asian region.
