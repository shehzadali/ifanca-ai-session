Back to All From Bimaristans to Modern Healthcare: Medical Pioneers of the Islamic World Yahya Arastu and Masuma Rizvi Resources June 30, 2026 Copied! You may have heard of Alexander Fleming, but have you ever heard of Al-Zahrawi? Fleming’s discovery of penicillin in 1928 transformed the treatment of bacterial infections and ultimately saved millions of lives. Yet his breakthrough was part of a much longer history of medical discovery. Centuries earlier, physicians and scholars working across the Islamic world inherited, translated, and expanded medical knowledge from Greek, Persian, Indian, and other traditions. Among them was Al-Zahrawi, a pioneering surgeon who documented the use of catgut, an animal-based surgical thread used for internal stitches, and described more than 200 surgical instruments. “Across major centers of learning such as Baghdad, Cairo, and Córdoba, Muslim physicians advanced approaches to surgery, pharmacology, clinical observation, and ophthalmology, while contributing to the development of organized healthcare and medical education.” Their work influenced medicine in Europe and beyond for centuries, but many people today still do not know about their valuable contributions. Medical pioneers who shaped global healthcare Ibn Sina Ibn Sina, one of the most influential physicians in Islamic history, lived during the 10 th and 11 th centuries in Persia, near the city of Bukhara. His medical encyclopedia, The Canon of Medicine , covered a wide range of topics, from anatomy, disease, and diagnosis to medicines, diet, and treatment. The work was used in European universities for centuries, showing the far-reaching influence of Islamic medicine. Ibn Sina also wrote about contagious and infectious diseases, the connection between physical and mental health, medical ethics, and ways to evaluate medicines. His systematic approach to studying and classifying drugs also helped lay the foundation for modern pharmacology. Ibn Sina (Avicenna), historical illustration. Wikipedia Commons, public domain. Al-Razi Al-Razi, another important figure in medical history, lived in the 9 th and 10 th centuries and worked in what is now Iran and Iraq. He took a practical approach to medicine, relying on careful observation of patients and their symptoms. His Comprehensive Book on Medicine brought together case studies, pharmacology, clinical observations, and information on medical treatment. He is best known for distinguishing smallpox from measles and describing the differences in their symptoms and treatments. He also stressed the importance of testing medicines before giving them to patients, an approach that remains an important part of modern medicine. Al-Zahrawi Al-Zahrawi lived and practiced in Córdoba, in present-day Spain, and is known as the “father of modern surgery.” In the 10 th century, he wrote Al-Tasrif , a thirty-volume encyclopedia on his surgical methods, procedures, and instruments. He illustrated hundreds of surgical instruments and detailed their uses, some of which are still used today, such as scalpels, forceps, catheters, and needles, and other obstetric and gynecological tools. He revolutionized surgical techniques, including cauterization for treating wounds and methods for setting fractures, and described the use of herbal mixtures for pain relief and sedation. He also emphasized how vital recovery was after a procedure and made significant contributions to the fields of dentistry and orthopedics. His writings were later translated into Latin and became a source of surgical education in Europe for centuries following his discoveries. Ibn al-Haytham In Iraq and Egypt during the 11 th century, Ibn al-Haytham wrote the Book of Optics , which explored the field of ophthalmology, especially his theory that sight occurs when light enters the eye instead of when something is projected outwards from it. He supported his theories through observation and experimentation, studying the behavior of light, reflection, and refraction, as well as the anatomy of the eye. His work helped establish an experimental approach to understanding vision and optics and later influenced the development of these fields in Europe. Ammar al-Mawsili An ophthalmologist from Mosul who practiced in Egypt around the 10 th –11 th century, Ammar al-Mawsili made important advances to cataract surgery. In his Book of Choices in the Treatment of the Eye , he described a hollow metal instrument that he used to remove soft cataracts through suction, rather than simply pushing the cataract out of the visual axis using the older technique of “couching.” He documented several cataract operations and emphasized adapting surgical techniques to individual patients. Ibn al-Nafis In the 13 th century, Ibn al-Nafis made an important contribution to anatomy and physiology through his description of pulmonary circulation—the movement of blood between the heart and lungs. In his Commentary on Anatomy in Avicenna’s Canon , he described blood traveling from the right side of the heart through the lungs before reaching the left side, challenging prevailing ideas about how blood moved through the heart. His description came centuries before similar ideas about pulmonary circulation were established in European medicine, making him an important figure in the history of physiology. Medical Ethics Hunayn ibn Ishaq, Al-Biruni, Ibn Zuhr, and Maimonides are other pioneers of Islamic medicine from the 9 th -12 th centuries who were not just known for their clinical and pharmacological advancements, but were renowned for their medical ethics. These included maintaining the patient’s confidentiality, having a compassionate and trusting relationship with the patient, caring for patients regardless of social status, and committing to lifelong learning throughout their time as practicing physicians. These values continue to be upheld today, with patients treated with dignity and respect and physicians trained on new medical advances throughout their careers. The Role of Women in Early Healthcare Women’s role in healthcare in early Islamic societies included midwifery, caregiving in hospitals, and providing medical support on the battlefield. Rufaidah al-Aslamiyyah, who lived in the 7 th century, is renowned for treating wounded soldiers. She also trained other women in emergency healthcare, contributing to the development of organized medical services. Inventions and hospital systems As much as healthcare discoveries created a long-lasting impact, the development of hospitals was one of the most enduring contributions from the Islamic medical era. “The bimaristans , hospitals established throughout the Islamic world in cities such as Baghdad, Cairo, and Damascus, provided free healthcare funded through charitable endowments.” They had specialized wards for different conditions, including mental health, ophthalmology, and surgery, as well as pharmacies, lecture halls, libraries, and gardens. Physicians continued their training and underwent examinations to practice in these hospitals, and they documented medical cases for educational and research purposes. We see some of these concepts reflected today in how medical students gain experience across different specialties through clinical rotations. The growth of healthcare in the Islamic world was also influenced by Islamic teachings that encouraged cleanliness, well-being, and medical treatment. Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) is reported to have said, “Make use of medical treatment, for Allah has not made a disease without appointing a remedy for it, with the exception of one disease, namely old age” ( Sunan Abi Dawud , 3855). Contemporary Relevance The contributions of medical pioneers from the Islamic world extend far beyond individual discoveries. Their work helped shape the development of hospitals, medical education, clinical practice, pharmacology, and ethical patient care. Many of these principles continue to resonate in healthcare today, from the way physicians are trained to the importance of treating patients with dignity and compassion. That legacy continues today, as careers in healthcare remain a source of pride in many families. “Muslim physicians, dentists, nurses, pharmacists, and other healthcare professionals serve communities around the world, continuing a tradition of service and scholarship that spans centuries.” As modern healthcare becomes increasingly inclusive, greater attention is also being given to patients’ cultural and religious needs, including halal medication and supplement ingredients, accommodations for those who fast during Ramadan, and respect for modesty in clinical settings. From Ibn Sina and Al-Zahrawi to today’s healthcare professionals, the history of medicine is a story shaped by contributions across cultures, faiths, and generations. Recognizing this shared history gives us a more complete understanding of how modern healthcare evolved—and the many people who helped shape it.
